#ARCHITECTURE AUDIT: The verdict: mostly not overengineered — the complexity is real, and Mids proves it  

The single most useful number from this comparison: MidsReborn is ~162,000 lines of C#, and it doesn't even attempt the hardest thing your project does. Its effect model is a 94-property interface with ~88 effect types across 83 enums; its calculation engine (clsToonX.cs) is 3,803 lines — by pure coincidence, your character-totals.ts is also exactly 3,803 lines. Two independent teams, two decades apart, converged on the same size for the same math. That's strong evidence the complexity is in the game, not in your architecture.  

And here's the part that should reframe your "vastly more complicated than I imagined" feeling: Mids has no data pipeline at all. There is no game-binary parser anywhere in that repo. Its .mhd database is hand-maintained by a volunteer team using an in-app database editor (~40 editing forms), then packaged and pushed through a patch server at [updates.midsreborn.com](http://updates.midsreborn.com/). When Homecoming patches the game, humans re-enter the numbers. Your pigg → Bin Crawler → export → convert pipeline, the DSH validation harness, the oracle diffs, GAME-DATA-PRINCIPLES.md — all of that is automation replacing an entire volunteer data team. You took on the part of the problem Mids outsourced to human labor. Of course it's bigger than you imagined; it was previously several people's ongoing job.  

The one genuine architectural mistake — which you already found  
There is one place where Mids got it right and Sidekick got it wrong, and it's exactly what your current migration is fixing. Mids models a power the same way the game does: a flat array of atomic effect records, each carrying its own type, magnitude, PvE/PvP mode, and resistibility, processed through a multi-pass pipeline (its "GBPA" passes: enhance → ED → post-ED → add → multiply). Your converter instead projects those atoms into the ~90 named single-value slots of PowerEffects with last-write-wins — which structurally cannot represent two sibling effects on the same stat. That's the documented root cause of the whole collapse-bug family, and every bolt-on fix (unresistable, durationVariants, domination) has been patching around the model rather than fixing it.  

So the DSH direction — build the atomic effect list first, project PowerEffects only at the end — is not just reasonable, it's converging on the design the game engine, the bin format, and Mids all share. Don't second-guess it mid-migration. The slightly painful irony is that the 20-year-old planner's data model was the thing worth copying; its everything-else was not.  

Where there IS trimmable accidental complexity  
A few things genuinely are heavier than they need to be:  

The overrides tree is ~90% empty scaffolding. Of 3,366 Homecoming override files, ~2,974 are empty {} stubs — times three datasets, that's roughly 9,000 placeholder files plus another ~3,700 thin composed-wrapper files per dataset. withOverrides could tolerate a missing override file, letting you delete every empty stub and every trivial wrapper. That's thousands of files gone with no behavior change.  
The same mistake was made three times. The powerset, incarnate, and IO-set converters each independently reinvented the bag-of-slots model, which is why DSH6, DSH8, and DSH9 all exist. Relatedly, your 11 incarnate-specific effect interfaces (AlphaEffects, HybridEffects, …) are parallel models that share nothing with PowerEffects — in Mids, incarnates are just powers flowing through the same effect pipeline. Post-DSH, unifying on AtomicEffect should let most of those interfaces and eventually convert-incarnate-effects.cjs itself dissolve.  
Powers carry both stats and effects and the calc falls back between them (power.stats?.recharge ?? power.effects?.recharge) — a small duplication, but it's a third parallel representation.  
What I would not call overengineering, despite the bulk: committing exported_powers/ (308MB) and generated/ (126k lines) with the byte-equality CI guard. That's a deliberate reproducibility decision with a written rationale, and given your history of silent data loss (the 265-power parser misalignment that three self-consistent layers all agreed on), the paranoid verification culture is earned, not ceremonial.  

Where you're architecturally healthier than Mids  
For balance: Mids' internals are in considerably worse shape than yours. Its main window is an 8,057-line God-form reaching into the character object 271 times; the calc engine directly calls MessageBox.Show() so it can't even run without WinForms; everything shares ambient mutable global singletons, making it near-untestable. You have strict TypeScript, a headless testable calc, CI regen guards, focused data regression tests, and written domain principles. Mids' equivalent of your DSH is "experienced volunteers eyeball the DB editor."  

Bottom line  
You're not overengineered in the sense of having built abstraction you don't need — you're carrying (a) irreducible domain complexity that Mids confirms, (b) an automated data pipeline Mids never built, (c) one wrong core data model that you've correctly diagnosed and are mid-way through fixing, and (d) some genuinely trimmable file-tree and parallel-model sprawl. My advice: finish DSH6 as planned, then let the atomic model earn its keep by deleting things — the incarnate interfaces, the duplicate converters, the empty override stubs, the stats/effects fallback. The end state you're heading toward is essentially Mids' proven calculation architecture, in a testable modern stack, with the data team replaced by a parser. That's the right destination.
